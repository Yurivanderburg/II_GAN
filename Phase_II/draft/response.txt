Comments to author:
The manuscript explores a conditional generative model for reconstructing simulated images of fast-rotating stars from sparsely sampled intensity-interferometry data. I found the central motivation appealing. If such a method can be validated against realistic II data, it could provide a useful complement to parametric modelling and may ultimately be relevant to stellar distortions and asymmetries arising from rotation, tides, companions, or magnetic activity. The present study is therefore a worthwhile first step. My comments below are essentially around reconciliating the github repository with the actual paper text. There is as well more minor recommendations or remarks.

Response: We sincerely thank the referee for the encouraging assessment of our work and for recognizing the potential of the proposed conditional generative model for reconstructing stellar surface images from sparsely sampled intensity-interferometry (II) data. We particularly appreciate the referee's observation that the method could provide a useful complement to parametric modelling and may have broader applications to stellar distortions and asymmetries arising from rotation, tides, companions, and magnetic activity.
We also appreciate the referee's careful effort in comparing the GitHub repository with the manuscript. We have thoroughly reviewed the manuscript and the accompanying GitHub repository to ensure consistency between the methodology, equations, descriptions, and the publicly available implementation. We have addressed the specific discrepancies and minor issues raised by the referee and made the corresponding corrections and clarifications in the revised manuscript and repository.


Important issues.

1. Relationship between the paper and the public code. Could the authors clarify which configuration produced the reported 6- and 9-IACT results? The manuscript’s abstract, figures, and results consistently discuss N_T = 6 and N_T = 9, whereas Table 1 gives “Number of Telescope: 4”. The public repository linked through Zenodo contains a four-telescope, VERITAS-like configuration (generate_baseline.py and generate_pspec.py) and supplied loss records for two, three, and four telescopes, but not six or nine. This may well be an incomplete release or a simplified example rather than the exact analysis code. It would be very helpful if the authors could reconcile Table 1 and provide the telescope positions, observing setup, and scripts or configuration files used for the two reported cases.

Response: We thank the referee for identifying this inconsistency. The value “4” in Table 1 was retained from an earlier configuration and does not correspond to the configurations used for the reported results. We have corrected Table 1 to reflect the N_T=6 and N_T=9 configurations. We have also updated the public repository to include the corresponding telescope configurations, observing setup, and relevant configuration files used for generating the results presented in the manuscript. These updates have been made to ensure consistency between the manuscript and the publicly available code and to improve the reproducibility of the reported results.


2. Noise model and II realism. The manuscript usefully develops the II SNR expression in Section 3.2. Could the authors explain how this expression enters the simulations? In the public code, salt-and-pepper perturbations seems to be applied to the stellar image before its FFT, and an additional perturbation is applied in the discriminator; this creates a noisy training input, but it does not appear to model uncertainty in a photon-correlation measurement at a stated per-baseline SNR. It would strengthen the physical interpretation to show results across a small set of realistic II SNR/noise conditions, or alternatively to frame the present results explicitly as a noiseless or phenomenologically perturbed proof of concept. The latter would still be useful.

Response: We thank the referee for this important comment regarding the physical interpretation of the noise model and we agree with it.
The SNR expression presented in Section 3.2 provides the theoretical basis for assessing the sensitivity of II measurements; however, it was not explicitly used to generate the noise in the simulations presented in the original manuscript. We have therefore clarified the distinction between the theoretical SNR analysis and the phenomenological perturbations used in the present computational framework at the end of section 3.2.
We have also clarified the role of the perturbations introduced during image preprocessing and discriminator training in section 5.1. We agree that a more physically realistic assessment should incorporate measurement uncertainties derived from the II correlation statistics and evaluate performance across representative baseline-dependent SNR levels. We identify this as an important direction for future work.


3. Evaluation protocol. Could the authors clarify the handling of the inherent 180-degree ambiguity? In the public code, optional_rotation() compares a prediction and its 180-degree rotation to the ground truth using MSE and retains the closer one before saving output images; the moment script then reads those saved predictions. If this is the pipeline behind Fig. 10 and Table 2, the choice is understandable as a way to account for the physical degeneracy, but it should be described clearly because it uses the target at test time. 








It may also be useful to report a symmetry-aware metric, or both orientations, together with the number of test examples, uncertainties, and the image scale/normalization needed to interpret Table 2.

Response: We thank the reviewer for highlighting the need to clarify the treatment of the inherent 180 degree rotational ambiguity. We have expanded the Evaluation section to describe the MSE-based orientation-selection procedure implemented in our code. For each test example, the original predicted image and its \(180^\circ\)-rotated counterpart are compared with the ground truth, and the orientation yielding the lower MSE is retained.
We now explicitly acknowledge that this procedure uses the ground truth at evaluation time and that the resulting image and moment comparisons represent best-match agreement under the assumed rotational degeneracy, rather than an independent recovery of the absolute orientation. We have also clarified the implications of this selection for the moment analysis and revised the caption of Table 2 accordingly. In addition, we are providing the test-set size, image scale and normalization conventions, and appropriate uncertainty estimates to improve the reproducibility and interpretation of the reported results.


4. Synthetic stellar family and scope of the conclusion. The benchmark family is a useful controlled starting point. The public code implements a shaded projected ellipse with intensity (4 + |cos r_s|)/5, while the manuscript describes a linear gravity-darkening dependence and cites the classical gravity-darkening literature. Could the authors state the adopted brightness law explicitly and explain its physical relation to the cited models? This would help readers understand the scope of the test. The current data set contains smooth objects without spots, granulation, limb-darkening structure, or tidal deformation, so it may be prudent to distinguish the demonstrated fast-rotator benchmark from the interesting longer-term prospect of recovering more complex surface features. The broader scientific motivation could in fact be emphasized more strongly in this discussion.

Response: We thank the reviewer for this constructive comment. We agree that explicitly defining the adopted brightness prescription and clarifying the scope of the synthetic benchmark are important for interpreting the results.
In the revised manuscript, we state the brightness law used in the public implementation explicitly, I=(4+|\cos r_s|)/5, and define the associated variable. We also clarify its physical interpretation and distinguish this prescribed intensity distribution from classical gravity-darkening models, which relate the emergent flux or effective temperature to the local effective gravity. We ensure that the relationship to the cited literature is described accurately and that the adopted prescription is not presented as equivalent to a classical gravity-darkening law unless this equivalence is physically justified.
We also agree that the present synthetic family represents a controlled and idealized benchmark. The models consist of smooth projected elliptical stellar objects with prescribed brightness distributions and do not include starspots, photospheric granulation, limb-darkening structure, or tidal deformation. We therefore clarify that the current results demonstrate the performance of the proposed method for the adopted smooth fast-rotator benchmark, rather than establishing its ability to recover all forms of realistic stellar surface structure.
Finally, we strengthen the discussion of the broader scientific motivation. In particular, we explain how the present benchmark provides a controlled starting point for investigating the reconstruction of more complex stellar brightness distributions and shapes, with potential applications to rapidly rotating stars and interacting binary systems. We identify these extensions as directions for future work and ensure that the conclusions remain consistent with the physical assumptions and results demonstrated in the present study.


5. Model description, baselines, and reported parameters. Section 4 describes G(z/y) with a latent variable, while the public implementation appears to use a deterministic U-Net G(y) with inference-time dropout. Could the authors clarify whether the latent-variable formulation is intended as a general cGAN description or as the exact model used here? Since the abstract notes that this particular synthetic problem might be addressed through parameter fitting, would a small comparison with a parameter-fit or an L1-only U-Net baseline help establish the role of the adversarial component? Likewise, Section 6.3 introduces position angle, semi-major and semi-minor axes, and eccentricity, but does not report their recovered values; reporting size and oblateness errors could connect the validation more directly to the stated science goal.

Response: We thank the reviewer for these constructive suggestions. We agree that clarifying the implemented generator architecture, evaluating the contribution of the adversarial loss, and reporting errors in the recovered stellar parameters would strengthen the methodological description and scientific validation.
First, we clarify the distinction between the general conditional GAN formulation presented in Section 4 and the specific implementation used in this work. The implemented generator is a conditional U-Net, and we explicitly state whether an independent latent variable is supplied to the generator. We also describe the role of dropout during inference and avoid conflating this stochasticity with an explicit latent-variable input.
Second, we agree that comparison with a simpler baseline would help establish the role of adversarial training in the present synthetic reconstruction problem. We evaluate the feasibility of including an L1-only U-Net baseline under comparable data and training conditions. This comparison help determine whether the adversarial loss provides measurable improvements beyond pixelwise reconstruction. We also discuss direct parameter fitting as a relevant alternative for this controlled synthetic problem and clarify the scope of the conclusions accordingly.
Third, we agree that the validation should connect more directly to the recovery of astrophysically relevant stellar parameters. We therefore report quantitative errors in the recovered semi-major and semi-minor axes, position angle, and eccentricity, where these quantities can be reliably extracted from the reference and reconstructed images. These results complement the image-level evaluation and provide a more direct assessment of the method's ability to recover stellar geometry.


6. Fourier-plane registration and the 6T/9T comparison. Could the authors clarify how the angular scale of the simulated source image is mapped to the (u, v) coordinates sampled by the telescope arrays? A brief illustration, for one simulated source, that the sparse input pattern corresponds to the intended Fourier-plane samples would help readers assess and reproduce the forward model. Finally, Table 2 shows smaller scatter for N6 than N9 in every listed metric. Since the two configurations differ both in telescope number and array geometry, could the discussion state this comparison somewhat cautiously? The present comparison alone may not isolate the relative roles of coverage and baseline number.

Response: We thank the reviewer for these helpful suggestions. We agree that the mapping between the angular scale of the simulated source and the sampled Fourier plane should be described more explicitly to facilitate reproducibility.
We clarify the forward-model procedure, including the image angular pixel scale, the Fourier-transform convention, and the conversion of projected telescope baselines into spatial-frequency coordinates (u,v) using the observing wavelength. We also include an illustrative example for a representative simulated source, showing how the intended Fourier-plane samples correspond to the sparse measurements supplied to the reconstruction method. This make the registration between the synthetic source and the telescope-array sampling more transparent.
We also agree that the comparison between the 6T and 9T configurations requires cautious interpretation. Although Table 2 shows smaller scatter for the 6T configuration across the reported metrics, the configurations differ in both telescope number and array geometry, resulting in different Fourier-plane sampling patterns. The present comparison therefore does not independently determine the effects of telescope number, baseline number, and spatial-frequency coverage. We revise the discussion to emphasize that the observed performance is specific to the configurations tested, rather than evidence that a smaller array is intrinsically superior. We identify controlled comparisons of array geometry and Fourier-plane coverage as an important direction for future investigation.
