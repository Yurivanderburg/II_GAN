Comments to author:
The manuscript explores a conditional generative model for reconstructing simulated images of fast-rotating stars from sparsely sampled intensity-interferometry data. I found the central motivation appealing. If such a method can be validated against realistic II data, it could provide a useful complement to parametric modelling and may ultimately be relevant to stellar distortions and asymmetries arising from rotation, tides, companions, or magnetic activity. The present study is therefore a worthwhile first step. My comments below are essentially around reconciliating the github repository with the actual paper text. There is as well more minor recommendations or remarks.

Response: We sincerely thank the referee for the encouraging assessment of our work and for recognizing the potential of the proposed conditional generative model for reconstructing stellar surface images from sparsely sampled intensity-interferometry (II) data. We particularly appreciate the referee's observation that the method could provide a useful complement to parametric modelling and may have broader applications to stellar distortions and asymmetries arising from rotation, tides, companions, and magnetic activity.
We also appreciate the referee's careful effort in comparing the GitHub repository with the manuscript. We have thoroughly reviewed the manuscript and the accompanying GitHub repository to ensure consistency between the methodology, equations, descriptions, and the publicly available implementation. We have addressed the specific discrepancies and minor issues raised by the referee and made the corresponding corrections and clarifications in the revised manuscript and repository.


Important issues.

1. Relationship between the paper and the public code. Could the authors clarify which configuration produced the reported 6- and 9-IACT results? The manuscript’s abstract, figures, and results consistently discuss N_T = 6 and N_T = 9, whereas Table 1 gives “Number of Telescope: 4”. The public repository linked through Zenodo contains a four-telescope, VERITAS-like configuration (generate_baseline.py and generate_pspec.py) and supplied loss records for two, three, and four telescopes, but not six or nine. This may well be an incomplete release or a simplified example rather than the exact analysis code. It would be very helpful if the authors could reconcile Table 1 and provide the telescope positions, observing setup, and scripts or configuration files used for the two reported cases.

Response: The git release linked through Zenodo was an older version (our error, now fixed).  We have corrected Table 1 for the N_T=6 and N_T=9 configurations used in this study.
To improve reproducibility, we have also updated the public repository to provide the telescope positions and configuration files for both arrays, together with the relevant observing parameters and scripts used to generate the simulated baselines and observations. These materials clarify the relationship between the configurations described in the manuscript and the publicly available implementation. We have checked the revised table, figures, and accompanying code for consistency.



2. Noise model and II realism. The manuscript usefully develops the II SNR expression in Section 3.2. Could the authors explain how this expression enters the simulations? In the public code, salt-and-pepper perturbations seems to be applied to the stellar image before its FFT, and an additional perturbation is applied in the discriminator; this creates a noisy training input, but it does not appear to model uncertainty in a photon-correlation measurement at a stated per-baseline SNR. It would strengthen the physical interpretation to show results across a small set of realistic II SNR/noise conditions, or alternatively to frame the present results explicitly as a noiseless or phenomenologically perturbed proof of concept. The latter would still be useful.

Response: We agree that the theoretical SNR expression and the perturbations used in our simulations should be clearly distinguished. We have clarified this distinction at the end of Section 3.2. We have also clarified in Section 5.1 that the perturbations introduced during image preprocessing and discriminator training are phenomenological and are not calibrated to represent photon-correlation measurement uncertainties at a specified, baseline-dependent II SNR. Consequently, the present simulations should be interpreted as a proof of concept under the adopted synthetic-data and perturbation scheme, rather than as a demonstration of reconstruction performance under physically calibrated II measurement noise.
A more realistic assessment should incorporate measurement uncertainties derived from II correlation statistics and investigate reconstruction performance across representative baseline-dependent SNR levels. We identify this as an important direction for future work.



3. Evaluation protocol. Could the authors clarify the handling of the inherent 180-degree ambiguity? In the public code, optional_rotation() compares a prediction and its 180-degree rotation to the ground truth using MSE and retains the closer one before saving output images; the moment script then reads those saved predictions. If this is the pipeline behind Fig. 10 and Table 2, the choice is understandable as a way to account for the physical degeneracy, but it should be described clearly because it uses the target at test time. It may also be useful to report a symmetry-aware metric, or both orientations, together with the number of test examples, uncertainties, and the image scale/normalization needed to interpret Table 2.

Response: The evaluation considered both orientations and reported the better (closer to the ground truth) one.  We have expanded the Evaluation section to describe the mean squared error (MSE)-based orientation-selection procedure implemented in our code. We have also clarified the implications of this selection for the moment analysis and revised the caption of Table 2 accordingly. The evaluation is performed on 100 test images. To quantify the variability in reconstruction performance, we calculate the moment reconstruction error for each test image and report the mean error across all 100 examples, together with its standard deviation in Table 3. We have also clarified the image scale and normalization conventions used to calculate the moments, thereby improving the reproducibility and interpretation of the reported results.



4. Synthetic stellar family and scope of the conclusion. The benchmark family is a useful controlled starting point. The public code implements a shaded projected ellipse with intensity (4 + |cos r_s|)/5, while the manuscript describes a linear gravity-darkening dependence and cites the classical gravity-darkening literature. Could the authors state the adopted brightness law explicitly and explain its physical relation to the cited models? This would help readers understand the scope of the test. The current data set contains smooth objects without spots, granulation, limb-darkening structure, or tidal deformation, so it may be prudent to distinguish the demonstrated fast-rotator benchmark from the interesting longer-term prospect of recovering more complex surface features. The broader scientific motivation could in fact be emphasized more strongly in this discussion.

Response:  In the revised manuscript, we state the brightness law used in the public implementation explicitly, I=(4+|\cos r_s|)/5, and define the associated variable. We also clarify its physical interpretation and distinguish this prescribed intensity distribution from classical gravity-darkening models, which relate the emergent flux or effective temperature to the local effective gravity. We ensure that the relationship to the cited literature is described accurately and that the adopted prescription is not presented as equivalent to a classical gravity-darkening law unless this equivalence is physically justified.
Finally, we strengthen the discussion of the broader scientific motivation. In particular, we explain how the present benchmark provides a controlled starting point for investigating the reconstruction of more complex stellar brightness distributions and shapes, with potential applications to rapidly rotating stars and interacting binary systems. We identify these extensions as directions for future work and ensure that the conclusions remain consistent with the physical assumptions and results demonstrated in the present study.



5. Model description, baselines, and reported parameters. Section 4 describes G(z/y) with a latent variable, while the public implementation appears to use a deterministic U-Net G(y) with inference-time dropout. Could the authors clarify whether the latent-variable formulation is intended as a general cGAN description or as the exact model used here? Since the abstract notes that this particular synthetic problem might be addressed through parameter fitting, would a small comparison with a parameter-fit or an L1-only U-Net baseline help establish the role of the adversarial component? Likewise, Section 6.3 introduces position angle, semi-major and semi-minor axes, and eccentricity, but does not report their recovered values; reporting size and oblateness errors could connect the validation more directly to the stated science goal.

Response:  We clarify the distinction between the general conditional GAN formulation presented in Section 4 and the specific implementation used in this work. The latent-variable notation is introduced to describe the general conditional GAN framework.
We agree that the relatively simple synthetic source considered in this work could also be reconstructed using conventional parameter-fitting techniques. However, the motivation for exploring machine-learning-based reconstruction is not limited to this particular example. Our longer-term objective is to investigate the potential of these techniques for reconstructing more complex stellar intensity distributions, including sources with diverse surface features, using the improved baseline coverage expected from larger IACT arrays and future facilities such as CTAO. We acknowledge that direct comparisons with conventional parameter-fitting methods and an L1-only U-Net would provide a more quantitative assessment of the advantages of the proposed approach and the contribution of the adversarial component. Such comparisons, together with investigations involving more complex source morphologies, are beyond the scope of the present preliminary study. We have therefore revised the relevant statement in the abstract and clarified in the manuscript that the current results demonstrate an initial feasibility study rather than establish the superiority of GAN-based reconstruction over existing approaches.
We also agree that comparing the recovered source parameters, particularly the characteristic size and oblateness, would provide a more direct assessment of the scientific performance of the reconstruction. In the present study, we evaluated the reconstruction using 100 test images and presented the relationships between the image moments of the reconstructed and reference images in Section 6.3, rather than reporting the individual geometric parameters for every image. These moment-based comparisons provide a quantitative assessment of the recovery of the overall source morphology, including its spatial extent and elongation.



6. Fourier-plane registration and the 6T/9T comparison. Could the authors clarify how the angular scale of the simulated source image is mapped to the (u, v) coordinates sampled by the telescope arrays? A brief illustration, for one simulated source, that the sparse input pattern corresponds to the intended Fourier-plane samples would help readers assess and reproduce the forward model. Finally, Table 2 shows smaller scatter for N6 than N9 in every listed metric. Since the two configurations differ both in telescope number and array geometry, could the discussion state this comparison somewhat cautiously? The present comparison alone may not isolate the relative roles of coverage and baseline number.

Response:  We have clarified the mapping between the angular pixel scale of the simulated source images and the spatial-frequency coordinates (\(u,v\)), including the conversion of projected telescope baselines using the observing wavelength. Figure 4 has also been updated to illustrate the correspondence between the telescope-array baselines and the sampled Fourier-plane locations.
We also agree that the 6T/9T comparison should be interpreted cautiously. Although the 6T configuration shows smaller scatter across all metrics in Table 2, the two configurations differ in both telescope number and array geometry. Therefore, this comparison does not isolate the effects of telescope number or Fourier-plane coverage on reconstruction performance. We have clarified this limitation in the discussion and emphasized that the results apply only to the configurations investigated.
